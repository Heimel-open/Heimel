Beyond Outputs: Toward a Science of Internal Representations
Njål Gaute Solland
Working Draft v0.1 - July 2026

Abstract
Artificial intelligence has traditionally been evaluated through externally observable
behaviour, including benchmark performance, prediction accuracy, reasoning success, and
human preference. While these measures quantify the outcomes of computation, they provide
only indirect evidence about the internal representations that produce those outcomes.
Recent advances in mechanistic interpretability, particularly the identification of privileged
internal workspaces in large language models, suggest that internal representations are
becoming experimentally accessible. This development motivates a shift in scientific focus from
evaluating outputs toward characterizing the representations from which those outputs
emerge.
This paper argues that internal representations should be treated as a primary scientific object
of AI research. Rather than proposing a single metric or explanatory theory, we introduce a
representational framework in which representations are characterized through
complementary classes of observable properties, including structural, geometric, dynamic,
functional, semantic, and operational observables. Within this framework, the previously
proposed tau metric is reinterpreted as a structural observable rather than a complete
description of representation.
Building on this perspective, we propose the concept of a representational state space in which
reasoning is understood as trajectories through evolving internal representations, while
language is interpreted as a projection of those representations rather than the representation
itself. This formulation generates testable predictions concerning representational equivalence,
workspace formation, model alignment, and robustness across architectures.
The primary contribution of this paper is therefore not a new model of intelligence, but a
reframing of the scientific object itself. As internal representations become increasingly
measurable, we argue that future progress in artificial intelligence will depend not only on
evaluating what models produce, but on understanding how they internally organize,
transform, and preserve information during reasoning.

Research Questions
  •    RQ1: What is a representation?
  •    RQ2: Which properties of representations can be measured objectively?
  •    RQ3: Can representations be described independently of model architecture?
  •    RQ4: Can representational observables predict behaviour better than outputs alone?
Contributions
The primary contributions of this paper are: (1) a proposal to treat internal representations as a
primary scientific object of AI research; (2) an architecture-independent definition of
representations as computational states that preserve information relevant for future
computation; (3) a taxonomy of six complementary classes of representational observables; (4)
the introduction of a conceptual representational state space; (5) the hypothesis that language
should be understood as a projection of internal representations rather than the
representations themselves; and (6) a falsifiable experimental research program for
representational science.
Scope. This paper does not propose a new neural architecture, a new training algorithm, a new
optimization method, or a complete mathematical theory of intelligence. It proposes a
framework for studying internal representations directly.

1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has advanced at an unprecedented pace. Foundation models now solve
complex reasoning tasks, generate software, assist scientific discovery, and increasingly act as
autonomous agents. Despite this progress, the scientific evaluation of these systems remains
overwhelmingly focused on observable behaviour.
Models are compared through benchmark scores, prediction accuracy, reasoning performance,
human preference, calibration, safety evaluations, and downstream task success. These metrics
have driven remarkable progress because they are measurable, reproducible, and directly
relevant to applications. Yet they all share a common limitation: they evaluate the
consequences of internal computation rather than the computation itself.
This distinction is becoming increasingly important. Two models may produce nearly identical
outputs while relying on fundamentally different internal organizations. Likewise, models with
similar benchmark performance often exhibit different failure modes, robustness,
interpretability, and generalization behaviour. Observable behaviour therefore
underdetermines the internal computational processes that produced it.
Historically, this limitation has been unavoidable. Internal representations were largely
inaccessible, making outputs the only practical object of scientific investigation. Recent
advances in mechanistic interpretability are beginning to change this situation. Instead of
treating neural networks as opaque systems that can only be evaluated externally, researchers
can now directly examine portions of their internal computational structure. The identification
of privileged representational workspaces, causal circuits, sparse conceptual features, and
interpretable activation patterns suggests that internal representations are becoming
measurable scientific objects rather than purely theoretical constructs.
This transition resembles earlier developments in the natural sciences. Scientific progress often
accelerates when previously hidden state variables become observable. Classical
thermodynamics expanded into statistical mechanics once microscopic particle states became
mathematically accessible. Astronomy evolved from describing planetary motion to explaining
it through gravitational dynamics. In each case, science shifted from describing observable
outcomes to understanding the internal structures responsible for them.
Artificial intelligence may now be approaching a similar transition. Rather than asking only
whether a model produces the correct answer, we can increasingly ask how that answer
emerges from the organization and transformation of internal representations. This shift does
not replace behavioural evaluation; it complements it by introducing a deeper level of scientific
description.
The central argument of this paper is intentionally modest. We do not propose a new theory of
intelligence, a new learning algorithm, or a new architecture. Instead, we propose that the
primary scientific object of AI research should gradually shift from observable outputs toward
internal representations.
Within this perspective, outputs become observable consequences of representational dynamics
rather than the primary object of study. Language becomes one projection of internal
computation rather than cognition itself. Metrics such as tau become individual observables
that characterize specific aspects of representational organization rather than complete
descriptions of intelligence.
This perspective motivates three fundamental questions: What is a representation, independent
of any particular model architecture? Which properties of representations can be measured
objectively? Can a general scientific framework be developed for studying representations
across artificial and biological systems?

2. What Is a Representation?
Before proposing a science of representations, we must first define its primary object. Although
the term representation is widely used across artificial intelligence, neuroscience, cognitive
science, and machine learning, it often carries different meanings depending on context. In
deep learning, representations are commonly associated with embeddings or latent vectors. In
neuroscience, they are linked to neural population activity. In cognitive science, they frequently
denote internal models of the external world. These definitions overlap but are not equivalent.
Definition 1 (Representation). A representation is an internal state that preserves information
relevant for future computation.
Several aspects of this definition deserve emphasis. First, a representation is internal. It exists
within the computational system rather than in its observable outputs. It is therefore distinct
from language, actions, or predictions, although these may all be generated from it.
Second, a representation preserves information. Internal activity that has no influence on
future computation is not considered representational in this framework. Representations are
defined by their computational relevance rather than by their physical implementation.
Third, the definition is intentionally independent of any specific architecture. A representation
may correspond to an activation vector in a transformer, a recurrent neural state, a biological
neural population, or another computational substrate entirely. The framework concerns its
functional role rather than its implementation.
This definition separates representations from related concepts. A token is not a
representation; it is an observable symbol generated from one. An embedding is not necessarily
a representation; it is one possible implementation of an internal state. A neuron is not a
representation; it contributes to representations but rarely constitutes one in isolation.
Likewise, a representation should not be identified with a particular layer of a neural network.
Representations may span multiple layers, evolve continuously during computation, or emerge
dynamically through interactions among distributed components.
Principle 1 (Representational Primacy). Observable behaviour is a consequence of internal
representations; it is not the representation itself.
This principle has several consequences. Two systems may generate identical outputs while
relying on fundamentally different internal representations. Conversely, similar internal
representations may generate different outputs depending on language, modality, context, or
task constraints. Behaviour alone therefore provides an incomplete description of cognition.

3. A Taxonomy of Representational Observables
If representations are to become scientific objects, they must be measurable. A representation
cannot be fully characterized by a single scalar, just as a physical system cannot be
characterized by mass alone. Different observables reveal different aspects of the same
underlying object.
We propose six classes of representational observables: R = (S, G, D, F, M, O), where S denotes
structural observables, G geometric observables, D dynamic observables, F functional
observables, M semantic observables, and O operational observables. These are not six separate
representations. They are six projections of one representational state.
Figure 2. Taxonomy of representational observables. A representation is characterized by
complementary structural, geometric, dynamic, functional, semantic, and operational
observables rather than by a single scalar.
Structural observables describe how information is distributed. Examples include effective
rank, spectral entropy, singular-value spectra, participation ratios, and tau. The tau metric
belongs here. It measures structural coherence by examining how representational energy is
distributed across dimensions. A low tau indicates collapse into few dominant directions. A
high tau indicates diffuse distribution. Intermediate values may indicate structured flexibility.
But tau does not measure meaning. It does not identify what is represented. It tells us how
information is organized, not what the information refers to.
Geometric observables describe the shape and position of representations in state space.
Examples include subspace alignment, principal angles, manifold curvature, topology, and local
dimensionality. These observables help distinguish models that may have similar structural
coherence but different internal organizations.
Dynamic observables describe how representations evolve through computation. Examples
include trajectory stability, attractors, contraction, Lyapunov exponents, fixed points, and
transition dynamics. These observables are essential for studying reasoning, because reasoning
is not a static state. It is a trajectory.
Functional observables describe what a representation does. Examples include Jacobian
influence, causal effect on output, workspace participation, information broadcast, and
attention flow. A representation may be structurally present without being functionally
influential. Functional observables distinguish passive internal structure from computation
that actually affects behaviour.
Semantic observables describe what a representation is about. Examples include concept
identity, referential grounding, abstraction hierarchy, cross-modal invariance, and symbolic
correspondence. This is the hardest class of observables and also one of the most important. A
complete science of representations must eventually explain not only how representations are
organized, but how they acquire reference and meaning.
Operational observables describe how representations lead to action. Examples include policy
stability, decision invariance, confidence, execution consistency, and action selection. This class
is especially important for agentic AI systems. A representation matters operationally when it
changes what the system does.
The central claim of this taxonomy is simple: a representation is not captured by one
observable. It is characterized by a family of observables. Tau is therefore not the theory. It is
one instrument in a broader representational science.

4. A Representational State Space
The taxonomy introduced in the previous section identifies measurable properties of
representations. The next question is whether these properties can be understood within a
common mathematical framework.
We propose the concept of a representational state space as a general abstraction for describing
internal computation. The purpose of this construct is not to specify a particular neural
architecture, but to provide a common language for reasoning about representations across
different computational systems. This proposal is conceptual rather than established theory.
Whether such a state space can be rigorously constructed, and whether it is unique, remain
open empirical and mathematical questions.
Figure 3. Representational state space. Reasoning can be interpreted as a trajectory through
evolving internal representational states, with operational fixed points corresponding to stable
decision regions.
Let R_t in R-space denote the internal representational state of a system at time t. Unlike
observable outputs, R_t is generally inaccessible directly. Instead, we observe projections of this
state through measurable quantities. Formally, O_i = Pi_i(R_t), where each projection operator
Pi_i produces one class of representational observables.
This distinction is fundamental. Observables are not the representation. They are
measurements of the representation. Just as temperature is not molecular motion itself but one
observable describing it, tau is not the representation itself but one observable describing its
structural organization.
Reasoning is naturally interpreted as the evolution of representations. Rather than viewing
inference as a sequence of tokens, we model it as a trajectory through representational state
space. In its most general form, R_{t+1} = F(R_t, E_t), where R_t is the current representation,
E_t denotes new evidence, context, memory, or external interaction, and F is the transformation
induced by the computational system.
Within this framework, language occupies a different role than in conventional evaluation.
Observable language is a projection of an internal representational state: L = P(R), where R is
the internal representation, P is a projection operator, and L is observable language.
Figure 4. Language as projection. Observable language is treated as a projection of an internal
representational state, not as the representation itself.
The projection hypothesis naturally introduces representational degeneracy. Two
computational systems exhibit representational degeneracy if they produce indistinguishable
observable behaviour while occupying different regions of representational state space.
Symbolically, P(R_1) = P(R_2) while R_1 != R_2.
Figure 5. Representational degeneracy. Distinct internal representations may project to the same
observable language or output, implying that behavioural equivalence need not imply
representational equivalence.
If representational degeneracy is common, behavioural evaluation alone cannot uniquely
identify the computational mechanisms underlying intelligence. This provides one explanation
for why models with similar benchmark performance often differ substantially in robustness,
interpretability, transfer learning, or failure modes.

5. Representations Before Language
Current language models are typically evaluated through generated text. Consequently,
language often becomes the implicit proxy for internal cognition. This identification is
understandable from an engineering perspective, since language is directly observable while
internal representations are not.
However, recent developments in mechanistic interpretability motivate an alternative
perspective. Evidence increasingly suggests that intermediate computational states exist before
they are verbalized. Internal workspaces, distributed circuits, latent manifolds, and causal
activation patterns all indicate that substantial computation occurs prior to language
generation. Observable text therefore appears to reflect only part of the underlying
computational process.
Hypothesis 1 (Representations Before Language). Internal representations are the primary
computational substrate of reasoning. Language is generated from those representations rather
than constituting the representations themselves.
This hypothesis does not imply that language is unimportant. Language provides an
exceptionally efficient interface for communicating internal representations between
individuals and artificial systems. The claim is simply that communication should not be
confused with computation.
One possible interpretation is that language functions as a compressed projection of a richer
internal representational state. The internal state may contain information that is never
verbalized because it is unnecessary, redundant, or impossible to express within the constraints
of natural language. Observable language therefore cannot uniquely reconstruct the internal
representational state.
This observation introduces a distinction between behavioural equivalence and
representational equivalence. Behavioural equivalence means that two systems produce
indistinguishable outputs. Representational equivalence means that two systems occupy
similar regions of representational state space. Behavioural equivalence does not necessarily
imply representational equivalence.
If representations are primary, output benchmarks become indirect measurements. They
remain valuable, but they cannot uniquely characterize the computational organization
responsible for successful reasoning. Future evaluation may therefore combine traditional
behavioural benchmarks with direct measurements of representational observables.
Rather than asking only whether the model solved the problem, we may increasingly ask what
internal representation enabled that solution.

6. An Experimental Program for Representational Science
A scientific framework is valuable only if it generates empirical predictions that can be tested,
reproduced, and potentially falsified. The purpose of this section is not to validate the
representational framework proposed in this paper, but to demonstrate that it produces
concrete experimental questions.
Figure 6. Experimental program. Representational science complements behavioural evaluation
by measuring internal states, extracting observables, and testing whether these predict
robustness, alignment, and behaviour.
Experiment I: Behavioral vs. Representational Equivalence. Can two models produce nearly
identical outputs while relying on different internal representations? Evaluate several frontier
and open-weight models on identical reasoning tasks. For each model, measure tau, effective
rank, principal subspace alignment, Jacobian influence, workspace participation, and
representational trajectories. Prediction: behavioural similarity will systematically
overestimate representational similarity.
Experiment II: Structural vs. Semantic Independence. Can structural organization remain
stable while semantic organization changes? Construct multiple tasks with similar structural
complexity but different conceptual content. Measure tau, geometry, and semantic observables.
Prediction: tau should remain relatively stable while semantic observables diverge,
demonstrating that structural coherence and semantic identity are distinct properties.
Experiment III: Representational Trajectories. Instead of measuring only final hidden states,
observe representational evolution throughout reasoning. Estimate sequences R_1, R_2, …,
R_n. Ask whether trajectories converge, whether different models follow similar paths, where
they diverge, and whether trajectory shape predicts reasoning quality.
Experiment IV: Language as Projection. Present identical reasoning problems using different
modalities: English, Norwegian, mathematics, source code, diagrams, and possibly images. If
representations are primary, representational observables should remain comparatively stable
even when linguistic realization differs substantially. Prediction: representational similarity
should exceed linguistic similarity.
Experiment V: Workspace Formation During Training. Monitor a model throughout training
and repeatedly estimate structural, geometric, functional, and workspace observables. Ask
whether structural coherence appears before functional workspaces, whether geometric
changes precede semantic organization, and whether there is a critical transition during
training.
Experiment VI: Cross-Architecture Comparison. Compare transformers, state-space models,
recurrent architectures, diffusion-based reasoning systems, and hybrid symbolic-neural
systems. Prediction: some representational observables will remain architecture-independent,
while others will prove architecture-specific.
The framework can be falsified if representational observables fail to predict robustness
beyond behavioural metrics, if models with identical outputs consistently exhibit
indistinguishable representational organization, if language uniquely determines internal
representations, or if no stable reproducible representational observables can be identified
across architectures.

7. Relationship to Existing Research
The framework proposed in this paper does not seek to replace existing theories of intelligence
or representation. Instead, it provides a common perspective from which several active
research directions can be understood as observing different aspects of the same underlying
phenomenon.
Mechanistic interpretability has transformed the study of neural networks by moving beyond
behavioural evaluation toward direct examination of internal computation. Research on sparse
features, activation steering, causal interventions, circuit analysis, and privileged internal
workspaces demonstrates that internal representations can increasingly be observed rather
than merely inferred. Within our framework, these approaches primarily measure functional
observables.
Information geometry studies probability distributions as geometric objects. It provides
mathematical tools for describing curvature, distance, and local structure. Representational
science naturally incorporates these methods, but treats geometry as one observable among
several rather than the complete description.
Global Workspace Theory proposes that conscious cognition emerges through the global
broadcasting of selected information. Recent observations of privileged internal workspaces in
language models suggest functional parallels. This paper deliberately avoids claims regarding
consciousness. Instead, we interpret workspaces operationally as regions of representational
state space with unusually high functional influence.
Modern neuroscience increasingly describes population activity through low-dimensional
neural manifolds. Similarly, large language models appear to organize information within
structured latent spaces. Manifolds become geometric descriptions of representational
organization rather than complete descriptions of representation.
Active Inference and world-model approaches emphasize internal models for adaptive
behaviour. These perspectives align naturally with representational science, but differ in
emphasis. Active Inference focuses on minimizing prediction error under uncertainty; world-
model approaches emphasize internal simulation and planning. Representational science
remains agnostic regarding optimization principles and focuses on describing representations
themselves.
The principal novelty of this paper is not a new mathematical observable or interpretability
technique. Instead, we propose three conceptual shifts: internal representations become the
primary scientific object rather than observable outputs; representations are characterized
through complementary observables rather than a single metric; and behavioural evaluation is
treated as one projection of representational organization rather than its complete description.

8. Limitations and Open Questions
The framework presented in this paper is ambitious in scope while preliminary in formulation.
It introduces a research program rather than a completed theory of internal representations.
First, this paper does not claim to provide a complete mathematical description of intelligence.
The representational state space and taxonomy of observables should be understood as
working hypotheses rather than established theoretical entities.
Second, there is no reason to assume that the proposed taxonomy is complete. Future research
may discover additional observables or demonstrate that some categories overlap or should be
subdivided.
Third, semantic measurement remains an open problem. Structural organization can often be
quantified mathematically. Geometry can be measured through manifold analysis. Functional
influence can be estimated through causal interventions. Semantic identity, however, remains
considerably more difficult to define objectively.
Fourth, architecture independence is not guaranteed. Different computational substrates may
require partially different representational descriptions. Determining which observables are
universal and which are architecture-specific is a major empirical objective.
Fifth, representations themselves are never observed directly. Only measurements derived from
them are accessible. This is common throughout science, but it means that all representational
claims remain model-dependent and subject to experimental revision.
Sixth, this framework intentionally avoids claims concerning consciousness. Internal
representations may prove important for understanding cognition without implying subjective
experience. Privileged internal workspaces should not automatically be interpreted as evidence
for consciousness.
Finally, the greatest scientific risk is that representations do not admit a unified description. It
is possible that different architectures organize internal computation in fundamentally
incompatible ways, or that behaviour remains a more reliable scientific object than
representations themselves. If future evidence supports either conclusion, the framework
should be revised or abandoned accordingly.

9. Discussion and Conclusion
The central contribution of this paper is not a new theory of intelligence. It is a proposal to
reconsider what artificial intelligence should study.
For much of its history, AI has necessarily focused on externally observable behaviour.
Benchmark performance, prediction accuracy, language generation, and downstream task
success have served as practical proxies for intelligence because internal computation was
largely inaccessible. Recent advances in mechanistic interpretability suggest that this historical
constraint is beginning to change.
As internal representations become increasingly measurable, they become legitimate scientific
objects in their own right. The central question therefore evolves from whether a model can
solve a task toward what internal representation made the solution possible.
Representational science should not be understood as competing with existing approaches.
Benchmark evaluation remains essential. Interpretability remains essential. Alignment
remains essential. Rather than replacing these fields, representational science provides a
common language through which they may become more closely connected.
Within this framework, the role of tau changes substantially. Originally introduced as a
measure of structural coherence, tau becomes one member of a broader family of
representational observables. This reinterpretation strengthens rather than weakens the
metric. Instead of asking tau to describe every aspect of representation, it is recognized as
measuring one specific property: structural organization.
If internal representations become routinely observable, several research directions naturally
emerge: standardized representational benchmarks, cross-model comparison based on
representational similarity rather than behavioural similarity, representation-aware training
objectives, representation-aware model evaluation, and cross-disciplinary comparisons
between biological and artificial representational systems.
Artificial intelligence has achieved remarkable progress through increasingly capable models
and increasingly sophisticated behavioural evaluation. This progress has transformed
engineering. It may also be preparing the next transformation in scientific understanding.
Throughout the history of science, major advances have often occurred when previously hidden
internal variables became measurable. Mechanistic interpretability suggests that artificial
intelligence may now be approaching a similar transition.
This paper argues that internal representations should become a primary scientific object of
study. Rather than proposing a single metric or a universal theory of intelligence, we have
outlined a conceptual framework in which representations are characterized through
complementary structural, geometric, dynamic, functional, semantic, and operational
observables.
The contribution is intentionally modest but potentially foundational. It does not claim to
explain intelligence. It proposes that understanding intelligence begins by studying the
representations from which intelligent behaviour emerges.
The twentieth century became the science of information. The twenty-first may become the
science of representations.
