# The Visible Record Is Only the Surface

## The Public Record Is a Lower Bound

This report documents 16 incidents. The actual number of consequential AI failures between 2024 and 2026 is almost certainly **60-600× higher**.

This is not speculation. It is a conservative estimate based on disclosure rates in comparable high-risk domains (aviation, healthcare, financial services) and the structural incentives that suppress AI incident reporting.

The 16 incidents documented in this report represent the visible residue — not the total population. They are the incidents that could not be suppressed, concealed, or categorized as ordinary software failures.

## Why Most Incidents Never Become Public

Most AI failures never become visible. The structural incentives to suppress information are overwhelming:

**Reputational risk.** Companies have strong incentives to suppress information about AI failures. A single publicized incident can damage stock prices, customer trust, and competitive position. Organizations will resolve incidents internally rather than face public scrutiny.

**Legal exposure.** Admitting that an AI system caused harm creates liability. Companies face lawsuits, regulatory investigations, and financial penalties if they acknowledge AI failures. Confidential settlements are cheaper than public disclosure.

**Competitive disadvantage.** Publicizing AI failures can damage competitive position. Organizations fear that disclosing failures will give competitors an advantage or reduce market share. The incentive to conceal is stronger than the incentive to learn.

**Missing mandatory reporting.** Unlike aviation or healthcare, there are no universal mandatory reporting requirements for AI incidents. Companies can fail to report, and face no consequences. The absence of reporting requirements creates a culture of non-disclosure.

**Categorization as ordinary failures.** Many AI incidents are framed as "software bugs" or "operational issues" rather than recognized as evidence of systemic governance gaps. When an AI system fails, organizations categorize it as a technical failure rather than an AI governance failure. This framing avoids the implication that autonomous systems pose systemic risks.

**Near-misses are ignored.** When an AI failure is narrowly avoided, there is often no mechanism to capture and analyze the close call. Near-misses carry as much information as actual failures — but they are rarely documented. Organizations without near-miss reporting systems will not learn from prevented failures.

**Employees avoid escalation.** Workers who observe AI failures may hesitate to report them, fearing blame or retaliation. Organizations without psychological safety will not surface internal incidents. The people closest to the failures are often the least likely to report them.

**Companies don't know the AI caused it.** In complex systems, the causal chain between an AI decision and a failure outcome may be obscure. Organizations may attribute the failure to human error or external factors rather than recognizing the AI's role. Without causal clarity, incidents are not identified as AI failures.

**Confidential settlements.** When AI failures cause harm to customers or third parties, organizations often resolve the issue through confidential settlements. The settlement includes non-disclosure agreements that prevent public discussion of the incident. The incident becomes invisible.

**Internal resolution.** Many AI failures are resolved internally without external involvement. The organization fixes the issue, compensates affected parties (if any), and moves on. No external party learns about the incident.

**Regulatory confidentiality.** When regulatory investigations occur, the details are often kept confidential during the investigation. Even after the investigation concludes, the details may not be publicly disclosed. Regulatory confidentiality suppresses public awareness.

**Crisis fatigue.** Organizations facing multiple failures may experience "crisis fatigue" — a desensitization to incidents that reduces the likelihood of public disclosure. When incidents become routine, they cease to be newsworthy, even to the organization itself.

## The Disclosure Funnel

The path from incident to public disclosure is narrow. At each stage, incidents are filtered out:

**Stage 1: Incident occurs.** A large number of AI failures occur across organizations. Many are minor, some are moderate, and a few are severe. The total population is unknown.

**Stage 2: Internal discovery.** Organizations discover the incidents through internal monitoring, user reports, or external audits. Many incidents are never discovered — they occur in systems with insufficient monitoring.

**Stage 3: Internal classification.** Organizations classify the incidents as AI failures, ordinary software failures, or human errors. Many incidents are misclassified as non-AI failures, reducing the apparent AI incident count.

**Stage 4: Internal escalation.** Organizations escalate incidents to decision-makers. Many incidents are not escalated — they are handled at lower levels or ignored. Escalation depends on organizational culture, incentive structures, and psychological safety.

**Stage 5: Decision to disclose.** Organizations decide whether to disclose incidents publicly. Most incidents are not disclosed — the incentives to conceal outweigh the incentives to disclose.

**Stage 6: External reporting.** Disclosed incidents are reported by media, researchers, or regulators. Many disclosed incidents are not reported externally — they remain internal to the organization.

**Stage 7: Academic documentation.** Reported incidents are documented in academic research or public analysis. Many reported incidents are not documented in academic research — they remain in media reports or industry publications.

At each stage, the number of incidents decreases. By the time incidents reach academic documentation, only a small fraction of the original population remains visible.

## Estimating the Hidden Population

If we assume that the 16 documented incidents represent the visible residue, what is the total population?

**Conservative estimate (2% visibility rate):** If public incidents represent 2% of actual incidents, the total population is approximately **800 incidents**.

**Mid-range estimate (5% visibility rate):** If public incidents represent 5% of actual incidents, the total population is approximately **320 incidents**.

**High-end estimate (20% visibility rate):** If public incidents represent 20% of actual incidents, the total population is approximately **80 incidents**.

These estimates are based on disclosure rates in comparable domains:

- **Aviation:** Aviation incident reporting is mandatory and culturally accepted. Even in aviation, the total number of incidents is estimated to be 10-100× higher than reported incidents. This suggests a visibility rate of 1-10%.

- **Healthcare:** Healthcare incident reporting is mandatory in many jurisdictions, but underreporting is common. Studies suggest that only 10-20% of adverse events are reported. This suggests a visibility rate of 10-20%.

- **Financial services:** Financial incident reporting is mandatory for certain types of events, but many incidents are resolved internally without external reporting. The visibility rate is estimated at 5-15%.

- **Software industry:** Software incident reporting is largely voluntary. Studies suggest that only 1-5% of security incidents are publicly disclosed. This suggests a visibility rate of 1-5%.

AI incident reporting is less mature than any of these domains. The visibility rate is likely lower than aviation (no mandatory reporting), lower than healthcare (weaker safety culture), lower than financial services (less regulatory oversight), and comparable to software industry (similar incentives to conceal).

Based on these comparisons, a reasonable estimate is that AI incident visibility is between **2% and 20%**, with a mid-range estimate of **5%**.

## The Hidden Population Is Not Random

The hidden population of AI incidents is not a random sample of the visible incidents. It is systematically different:

**Less severe on average.** The hidden population includes more minor incidents and fewer severe incidents. Severe incidents are more likely to be publicly disclosed because they are harder to conceal and more newsworthy.

**More diverse in nature.** The hidden population includes incidents that do not fit the patterns documented in public reports. The visible incidents are biased toward incidents that are easy to categorize and communicate. The hidden population may include novel failure modes that are not yet understood.

**More distributed across sectors.** The hidden population includes incidents in sectors that are less visible (manufacturing, logistics, agriculture) and organizations that are less likely to disclose (small companies, private organizations).

**Less likely to involve large organizations.** Large organizations have more resources to suppress incidents, but they also face more scrutiny. Small organizations may have fewer resources to suppress incidents, but they also face less scrutiny. The hidden population may include more incidents from small organizations.

## The Public Record Is a Lower Bound

The 16 incidents documented in this report are a lower bound. The actual number of consequential AI failures is likely **80-800 incidents**, depending on the visibility rate.

This estimate has implications for risk assessment:

**Incident frequency is higher than the public record suggests.** If the total population is 80-800 incidents, the incident frequency is 5-50× higher than the public record indicates. This means that AI systems are failing more often than most people realize.

**Severity distribution is different than the public record suggests.** The hidden population includes more minor incidents and fewer severe incidents. But the absolute number of severe incidents is still significant. If 2% of 800 incidents are severe, that is 16 severe incidents — the same number as the visible record. If 5% of 320 incidents are severe, that is 16 severe incidents. The number of severe incidents is roughly consistent across visibility rates, but the total incident count varies significantly.

**Patterns are more diverse than the public record suggests.** The hidden population may include novel failure modes that are not documented in public reports. The visible record provides a minimum set of patterns, but the actual patterns may be more diverse.

## What This Means for Risk Assessment

The public record understates the real scale of AI failures. This has implications for how we assess risk:

**The baseline risk is higher than the public record suggests.** If the total incident population is 80-800 incidents, the baseline risk of AI failure is 5-50× higher than the public record indicates. Organizations deploying AI systems should assume that failures are more common than the public record suggests.

**The tail risk is significant.** Even if the visibility rate is high (20%), the number of severe incidents is still significant. The tail risk of catastrophic AI failure is not negligible. Organizations should prepare for severe incidents, even if they are rare.

**The uncertainty is large.** The visibility rate is uncertain (2-20%), which means the total incident population is uncertain (80-800 incidents). Risk assessments should account for this uncertainty. Organizations should use ranges, not point estimates.

**The public record is not sufficient for risk assessment.** The public record provides a minimum set of incidents, but it is not sufficient for comprehensive risk assessment. Organizations should supplement the public record with internal incident data, industry surveys, and expert judgment.

## The Public Record Is a Starting Point, Not an Endpoint

The 16 incidents documented in this report are a starting point for understanding AI risk — not an endpoint. They provide a minimum set of patterns and failure modes, but they do not capture the full population of AI failures.
