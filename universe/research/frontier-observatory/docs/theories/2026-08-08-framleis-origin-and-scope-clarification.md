# Framleis — Origin and Scope Clarification

**Date:** 2026-08-08  
**Status:** Canonical clarification for Framleis v2.0; input to a future v2.1 revision  
**Author:** Njål Gaute Solland  
**Tracking:** Tofoo issue #85

---

## 1. Why this clarification exists

Framleis v2.0 records a four-agent Panoptikon experiment, but it compresses a broader historical lineage and later broadens the language in a way that can make the experimental architecture read as a direct model of human cognition.

Both points need correction.

This note therefore restores two things:

1. the actual development path from the ecovillage resilience problem into the LLM multi-agent society, including Panoptikon, Janus and the outer Kimi barrier;
2. the scope boundary between the Framleis core hypothesis, the experimental multi-agent architecture, and later analogies to human cognition.

The v2.0 manuscript is preserved as a historical research artifact. This clarification does not rewrite that record in place.

---

## 2. The actual origin: a resilience question, not a cognition model

The work did not begin with an attempt to model the human mind or to build an AI company.

The starting question was:

> **What makes a society resilient?**

The first model was an ecovillage, understood not primarily as buildings or infrastructure but as a persistent society.

The questions were social and systemic:

- How does knowledge survive the people who currently hold it?
- How is trust created and repaired?
- How are responsibilities distributed?
- How does a community preserve accumulated wisdom?
- How does it adapt to change without losing continuity or identity?
- What happens when members enter, leave, fail, die or are replaced?
- How does the community retain what matters without becoming trapped by its own history?

When large language models became practically useful, the next experiment was to place this societal model inside an LLM-based multi-agent environment.

The question became:

> **Can intelligent agents organise themselves into a persistent community, and what mechanisms are required for that community to remain coherent over time?**

This experiment, not a theory of individual human cognition, is the direct origin of the Framleis architecture.

---

## 3. What the simulated society exposed

The multi-agent society immediately revealed persistence failures that ordinary single-turn interaction hides:

- agents forgot;
- context disappeared;
- knowledge drifted across iterations;
- identity was not reliably persistent;
- information, knowledge and wisdom were insufficiently distinguished;
- accumulated experience did not automatically survive individual agents;
- adaptation could destroy continuity;
- preservation could create rigidity;
- external input could overwhelm the society with noise before useful state could be established.

Development proceeded by solving these failures one at a time.

The architecture therefore did not begin as a predesigned four-part cognitive theory. Roles and boundaries emerged because the simulated society encountered concrete problems that required a function to exist.

---

## 4. The broader origin architecture: Panoptikon, Kimi and Janus

The surviving v2.0 paper foregrounds Nova, Lumi, Janus and Aethel. That four-agent description is useful, but it is an incomplete compression of the broader ecovillage / Panoptikon lineage.

Three boundary roles are especially important to restore.

### Panoptikon — collective observation

Panoptikon was not merely a decorative name for the simulation.

Its core role was observation: maintaining a collective field in which the system could see what was happening across the society rather than relying on the partial view of one actor.

In the simplest formulation:

> **Panoptikon observes.**

It represents the problem of shared situational visibility before the society can decide what to preserve, change or act upon.

### Kimi — outer barrier / noise sink

Kimi belonged at the outer edge of the original system.

Its purpose was to absorb the unstructured external noise hitting the society so that every disturbance did not propagate directly into the internal agent system.

Kimi should therefore be understood historically as an **outer protective/noise boundary**: a buffer between an uncontrolled environment and the more structured internal society.

This is not the same function as Janus.

Kimi takes the noise at the outside. Janus governs passage and stability at the meaningful threshold inside the governed system.

### Janus — threshold / passage / stability

Janus is the gatekeeper role.

It emerged around the question:

> **What may pass from one state into the next without destroying coherence?**

Janus therefore represents filtering, threshold control, transition and stability. It is not simply a generic "negative-memory filter" and should not be reduced to that description.

A useful historical distinction is:

> **Panoptikon observes. Kimi absorbs outer noise. Janus governs passage.**

This three-part distinction must be preserved in future origin descriptions.

---

## 5. The four-agent Panoptikon experiment

Within the later four-agent experimental form, the canonical roles were:

### Nova — visibility / light

Nova maintained information accessibility and local visibility inside the agent society.

Nova and Panoptikon are therefore related but not identical. Panoptikon describes the broader collective observation architecture; Nova is a functional role inside the agent decomposition.

### Lumi — guidance / direction

Persistence alone is insufficient. A system can preserve itself perfectly while becoming purposeless or unable to choose among possible changes.

Lumi represents navigational direction and guidance in the artificial community.

### Janus — filtering / threshold / stability

Janus controls meaningful passage and stabilises transitions. What should pass? What should be held? Which proposed change would preserve coherence, and which would destabilise it?

### Aethel — heritage / continuity / collective memory

Aethel emerged from a specifically social problem:

> **What must survive when the current holder of knowledge no longer does?**

Aethel therefore represents heritage, continuity and preservation across actors.

Its natural unit is not necessarily the individual agent. Memory can be distributed across agents, relationships, records, institutions and shared practices.

This is important: Aethel should not be read as an analogue of a single human memory store.

---

## 6. The architecture grew beyond four agents

As the simulation developed, it incorporated mechanisms and roles for:

- shared observation;
- outer noise absorption and buffering;
- guidance and direction;
- threshold control and stabilisation;
- collective memory;
- wisdom preservation;
- inheritance;
- forgetting;
- cooperation;
- councils and distributed deliberative functions;
- agent entry, replacement, death and lifecycle transitions;
- transfer of knowledge and responsibility across generations of agents.

The four-agent representation should therefore be treated as one important experimental configuration, not the complete historical ontology of the ecovillage work.

Framleis grew out of a problem of **collective persistence under change**.

---

## 7. Distributed memory is native to the original problem

A later reading of Framleis can mistakenly assume that persistence means continuously accessible internal memory inside one agent.

The original ecovillage model points in a broader direction.

A persistent social system can retain history even when no single member retains all of it.

One actor may remember an event that another has forgotten. A record can preserve a commitment after all original participants are gone. A norm can survive through practice. A relationship can carry context not present in either party's explicit current recall.

Therefore:

> **Persistence does not require all relevant memory to reside inside one cognitive actor.**

For Framleis, distributed memory is not an exception. It is a natural consequence of the original social-system setting.

---

## 8. Correction to the v2.0 valence language

Framleis v2.0 describes valence-separated memory using language such as positive memories being amplified and negative or traumatic patterns being filtered or suppressed.

That language originated as an engineered simulation mechanism for regulating retention, propagation and system load.

It must not be treated as a universal theory of human memory.

Human memory supplies obvious counterexamples to a simple positive-amplification / negative-suppression account:

- recall can be strongly cue-dependent;
- inaccessible memories can become available after long intervals;
- negative events can persist more strongly than positive events;
- traumatic memories can become intrusive rather than filtered;
- different people can carry different parts of a shared history;
- internal representational experience varies substantially across people, including aphantasia.

These observations do not falsify the use of valence, retention, buffering or noise controls in an engineered multi-agent system.

They do falsify any unsupported claim that the same mechanism is a direct architectural description of human memory.

Future revisions should therefore describe the v2 valence model as an **experimental retention / salience / propagation policy** unless independent evidence supports a broader claim.

## 9. The three-layer scope of Framleis

### Layer A — Framleis Core

The most general research proposition is the tension between persistence and adaptation:

- complete preservation can produce rigidity;
- complete replacement can destroy continuity;
- adaptive systems require some mechanism that preserves sufficient structure while allowing meaningful change.

This is the core hypothesis to test independently of any particular agent decomposition.

### Layer B — Ecovillage / Panoptikon multi-agent architecture

Panoptikon, Kimi, Nova, Lumi, Janus, Aethel, valence handling, councils, inheritance, forgetting and lifecycle mechanisms are concrete experimental realisations developed in the ecovillage research lineage.

They are design heuristics and testable architectures for persistent artificial societies.

They are not universal ontological claims about every adaptive system.

### Layer C — Human / biological / social analogies

Mappings from Framleis into individual human cognition, neuroscience, biology or other domains are hypotheses.

They may reveal useful structural similarities, but similarity of function does not establish identity of mechanism.

Any such mapping must state:

- the level of abstraction;
- the proposed correspondence;
- the evidence for the correspondence;
- known counterexamples;
- what observation would falsify the mapping.

No human-cognition analogy should be promoted to architectural equivalence without independent empirical support.

---

## 10. Boundary with standing, truth and authority

Framleis concerns transformation, persistence, adaptation and stabilisation.

A representation becoming stable under Framleis-like iteration does **not** establish that it is:

- true;
- properly identified;
- evidentially supported;
- authoritative;
- admissible;
- permitted to cause an external action.

Accordingly:

> **Stability is not standing. Persistence is not truth. Convergence is not authority.**

Any governed architecture using Framleis must preserve or explicitly record changes to the standing, basis and unresolved state of what is being transformed.

Execution authority remains a separate downstream question.

## 11. Editorial rules for Framleis v2.1

### MUST

1. Add the ecovillage-to-LLM multi-agent origin before introducing the later four-agent experiment.
2. Restore Panoptikon as the collective observation architecture rather than treating it only as the experiment's name.
3. Restore Kimi as the historical outer barrier/noise-buffer role.
4. Describe Janus as threshold/passage/stability, not merely a negative-memory filter.
5. State explicitly that Nova, Lumi, Janus and Aethel are functional roles that emerged from the simulated society.
6. Separate the Framleis core hypothesis from particular Panoptikon implementations.
7. Replace universal psychological language around valence with experimental AI-system language unless independently evidenced.
8. State that collective memory may be distributed across actors and external persistent structures.
9. Mark human-cognition mappings as hypotheses rather than architectural facts.
10. Preserve counterexamples instead of forcing them into the Framleis model.
11. Keep stabilisation separate from standing, admissibility and execution authority.

### MUST NOT

1. Present the four-agent decomposition as a literal model of the human mind.
2. Present the four-agent experiment as the complete historical architecture of the ecovillage work.
3. Collapse Panoptikon, Kimi and Janus into a single generic filtering function.
4. Treat negative human memory as noise that a healthy system simply suppresses.
5. Infer universality from the success of one engineered simulation architecture.
6. Treat convergence or persistence as evidence of truth or legitimacy.
7. Rewrite the historical v2.0 record as though these distinctions were always explicit.

---

## 12. Revised concise formulation

The current defensible formulation is:

> **Framleis began as an investigation into how a persistent multi-agent society can preserve continuity while adapting. The ecovillage lineage developed layered functions for collective observation, outer noise buffering, threshold control, direction, memory and inheritance. The later four-agent Panoptikon experiment is one experimental decomposition of that broader problem. The broader Framleis hypothesis concerns the balance between persistence, adaptation and stability. Applications to human cognition are comparative research hypotheses, not assumed architectural equivalences.**

A still shorter form is:

> **Framleis models persistence under change. The agents and boundaries emerged from a simulated society; they are not a map of the human mind.**

---

## 13. Research consequence

This clarification narrows one claim but restores a stronger research programme.

The relevant questions include:

- How can a society of agents preserve knowledge beyond individual lifetimes?
- How should an artificial society separate observation from noise absorption and meaningful passage?
- Which state must be internal, and which can be relational or institutional?
- How should memories become active, dormant, inherited, disputed or superseded?
- How can a system preserve contradictory or unresolved history without inventing coherence?
- What must remain invariant through transformation?
- Which kinds of forgetting improve adaptation, and which destroy accountable continuity?
- How can a system change its representation without silently changing what the representation is allowed to mean or support?

These questions are closer to the problem that originally produced Framleis and provide a cleaner basis for subsequent Tofoo research.
