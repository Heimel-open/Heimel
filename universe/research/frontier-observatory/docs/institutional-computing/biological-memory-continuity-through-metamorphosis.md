# Biological Memory Continuity Through Metamorphosis

Date: 2026-07-20
Status: Research reference and hypothesis source — not validation of Framleis or α = 0.42

## Summary

Research on learned avoidance surviving metamorphosis is relevant to Framleis because it indicates that action-relevant information can persist through radical biological reconstruction.

The strongest defensible interpretation is not that an unchanged memory object survives intact. It is that a selective constraint on later behaviour persists or is reconstructed across a major state transition.

This supports Framleis as a theory of continuity through transformation. It does not validate the numerical parameter α = 0.42.

## Jo Nagai research presented at ICE 2024

Jo Nagai presented a poster at the International Congress of Entomology in Kyoto on 28 August 2024 titled:

> Swallowtail butterflies remember what they learned as caterpillars, and the sex-linked memories persist for three generations.

The work was developed with advice and statistical support from Professor Martha Weiss at Georgetown University. It has been described in a 33-page report and through the Radiolab / Signal Hill episode `Caterpillar Roadshow`, but has not been located as a conventional peer-reviewed journal article.

The reported experiment associated lavender odour with mild electrical stimulation delivered using a consumer muscle-therapy device. Later experiments reported that a substantial majority of the adult butterflies avoided lavender after metamorphosis. The podcast describes results around 70–80 percent depending on the experimental round and analysis.

The claimed transfer of the learned response into later generations is potentially important, but must remain provisional until the complete methods, raw data, peer review and independent replication are available.

## Established supporting research

A peer-reviewed 2008 study by Martha Weiss and colleagues showed that learned odour aversion acquired late in caterpillar development could remain observable in adult moths after metamorphosis. Learning acquired earlier did not survive in the same way.

This indicates selective continuity:

- not every learned state persists
- timing and developmental state matter
- persistence is filtered rather than complete
- behavioural continuity does not require preservation of the organism's full prior form

Recent neural-development research also shows that metamorphosis is not a complete dissolution of all cells and neural structures. Some neurons and cellular identities survive and are remodelled. Other connections may be replaced or reconstructed. Therefore, the evidence does not establish one specific physical storage mechanism.

## Comparative organisms and continuity modes

The phenomenon is not limited to butterflies. Related effects appear across several kinds of organisms, but they represent different mechanisms and must not be treated as one identical process.

### Frogs — embryonic learning through metamorphosis

Frog embryos exposed to specific odours later showed corresponding preferences as tadpoles. Individuals that retained the preference as tadpoles also retained it after metamorphosis.

Continuity mode:

`embryo → tadpole → adult frog`

Relevance:

- information acquired before a mature nervous system remains behaviourally relevant later
- the preference crosses major changes in body form and sensory organisation
- persistence is individual and selective rather than universal

Evidence status: peer-reviewed experimental result.

### Houseflies — larval olfactory experience into adulthood

Larval *Musca domestica* exposed to mint or geraniol later showed odour-specific adult preferences after complete metamorphosis. A second experiment reported that grafted cells from trained donors altered odour preferences in untrained recipients.

Continuity mode:

`larval exposure → complete metamorphosis → adult preference`

Relevance:

- suggests that specific cellular or molecular states may carry information across metamorphosis
- the cell-grafting result is especially relevant to distributed or extra-synaptic storage hypotheses

Evidence status: peer-reviewed but based on an older single study; replication and modern mechanistic confirmation are needed.

### Planarian flatworms — latent memory through whole-brain regeneration

Planarians trained in an environmental familiarisation task retained memory for at least fourteen days. After decapitation and regeneration of a new head and brain, trained animals showed faster retrieval or relearning in a savings paradigm.

Later work using more than 20,000 behavioural trials reported long-lived latent memory states during whole-brain regeneration and implicated long-range peptidergic signalling in restoration of coarse behavioural states.

Continuity mode:

`trained organism → brain removal → brain regeneration → accelerated retrieval`

Relevance:

- memory-relevant state is not reducible to the original intact brain alone
- the body may preserve constraints that guide reconstruction of later neural behaviour
- the result concerns latent memory and savings, not perfect recall by a brain that no longer exists

Evidence status: peer-reviewed, replicated through improved high-throughput methods, with an emerging mechanistic account.

### C. elegans — learned avoidance across generations

*C. elegans* can learn to avoid pathogenic bacteria. Purified bacterial small RNAs can induce the avoidance response, which is then transmitted through the germline to several untrained generations.

Peer-reviewed studies identify specific small RNAs and host pathways required for this transfer. In some experiments, avoidance persists through four generations and disappears in the fifth. The inherited response can also become maladaptive when it causes avoidance of beneficial bacteria, offering a functional reason for controlled forgetting.

Continuity mode:

`parental experience → germline regulatory state → descendant behaviour`

Relevance:

- acquired information changes the future admissible behaviour of descendants
- the effect is regulatory and reversible rather than a permanent DNA-sequence mutation
- inheritance duration is itself controlled by biological feedback and inheritance states

Evidence status: strong peer-reviewed molecular and behavioural evidence.

### Physarum slime mould — memory without a nervous system

The unicellular slime mould *Physarum polycephalum* can habituate to sodium, normally a repellent. After training, conversion into a dormant sclerotium and revival one month later, the organism retained the habituated response. Chemical analysis showed that sodium accumulated during learning and remained through dormancy.

Continuity mode:

`active cell → learned chemical state → dormancy → reactivation`

Relevance:

- demonstrates a memory-like state without neurons or a brain
- the physical carrier can be an internal chemical concentration
- information remains latent while the normal active process is suspended

Evidence status: peer-reviewed with an identified candidate substrate.

### Locusts and other hemimetabolous insects

Some older experiments report learned responses persisting from nymph to adult in insects that develop without complete pupal reconstruction. This is relevant as a lower-intensity comparison because the nervous system is remodelled less radically than in butterflies or flies.

Continuity mode:

`nymph → final moult → adult`

Relevance:

- shows behavioural continuity across developmental transitions
- does not provide the same evidential force for persistence through complete metamorphosis

Evidence status: supporting comparison, not a central case.

## Negative and limiting evidence

The evidence does not show that learned states routinely survive every transformation.

In *Drosophila*, detailed mapping of mushroom-body circuits found that no larval input-output connection was anatomically maintained through metamorphosis, despite survival and repurposing of some cellular lineages. Other behavioural studies have failed to find secure larva-to-adult memory, and apparent effects can be confounded by odours retained on the pupal case or by non-associative exposure.

These negative results are important for Framleis:

- continuity is conditional
- the carrier may differ between species and tasks
- anatomical continuity is neither always sufficient nor always necessary
- reconstructed behaviour must be distinguished from persistence of an unchanged memory trace
- contamination, selection and developmental bias must be ruled out

## Comparative synthesis

The strongest cross-species pattern is not universal memory survival. It is selective persistence of action-relevant constraints through different kinds of discontinuity:

- butterfly or moth: metamorphic reconstruction
- frog: embryo-to-adult development
- housefly: complete metamorphosis
- planarian: removal and regeneration of the brain
- *C. elegans*: transfer through generations
- *Physarum*: dormancy without a nervous system

The possible carriers are correspondingly different:

- surviving or remodelled neurons
- persistent cellular identity
- reconstructed neural circuitry
- distributed peptide signalling
- epigenetic and small-RNA states
- cytoplasmic or germline signals
- retained chemical concentrations

Therefore, Framleis should not posit one universal memory substrate. The stronger proposition is substrate-independent continuity of constraints implemented by locally available mechanisms.

## Connection to Framleis

Represent the transition as:

`X_caterpillar → metamorphosis → X_butterfly`

The organism changes radically, but a learned behavioural constraint can remain observable:

`lavender → avoid`

The relevant continuity is therefore not material sameness. It is persistence of a relation that constrains future state transitions and actions.

This supports the following Framleis proposition:

> Identity can persist when a system preserves or reconstructs the constraints that determine which future states remain admissible.

The evidence is consistent with identity as selective informational continuity through transformation:

- structure changes
- embodiment changes
- many local states disappear
- a behaviourally relevant exclusion remains
- the later system is partly shaped by what it has learned not to enter or approach

This is closely aligned with the working statement:

> Identity is memory of what is filtered out.

The biological case should be treated as an empirical analogy and hypothesis source, not as direct proof of the full theory.

## Latent regulatory memory, not latent genes

The phrase `latent genes` is too strong and technically misleading unless a specific inherited genetic sequence change is demonstrated.

Use instead:

- latent regulatory memory
- inherited constraint state
- transgenerational behavioural bias
- epigenetic or small-RNA-mediated continuity

Possible carriers include:

- persistent neural or cellular identity
- reconstructed neural circuitry
- chromatin states
- DNA methylation
- histone modification
- small RNAs
- hormonal or cytoplasmic signals transmitted through eggs

A related peer-reviewed result in *C. elegans* shows that learned pathogen avoidance can be transmitted for several generations through small-RNA-dependent regulation. This demonstrates that an acquired behavioural constraint can be inherited without becoming a permanent DNA-sequence change.

## Relation to α = 0.42

These studies do not validate α = 0.42.

The reported 70–80 percent avoidance rates are proportions of organisms selecting or avoiding an odour. They are not measurements of:

- retained representation
- state-update weight
- contraction rate
- coherence retention
- the proportion of prior identity preserved through transformation

No defensible mathematical mapping exists from the reported behavioural percentages to α.

The evidence supports the qualitative mechanism required by Framleis: partial and selective continuity across transformation. It does not determine the quantitative update coefficient.

## Testable research direction for α

To test whether α ≈ 0.42 has biological relevance, future work would need repeated measurements across:

1. training strength
2. developmental timing
3. metamorphic stages
4. adult behavioural response
5. neural or molecular state markers
6. successive generations

A state-transition model could then estimate how much prior constraint is retained versus overwritten at each transition. α would need to emerge as a fitted, stable parameter across datasets rather than being inferred from one avoidance percentage.

## Falsification boundaries

This reference weakens rather than supports Framleis if later evidence shows that:

- the adult behaviour is caused entirely by uncontrolled odour exposure or selection effects
- the learning effect does not replicate
- the reported generational transfer disappears under blinded and preregistered testing
- continuity requires complete preservation of a specific physical structure rather than persistence or reconstruction of constraints
- no stable relationship can be found between retained constraints and later identity or behaviour

## Adoption into Tofoo

Adopt as:

- empirical support for selective continuity through radical transformation
- evidence that behavioural exclusions may outlast the structures in which they were acquired
- a biological model for latent regulatory constraints
- a source of falsifiable experiments for Framleis
- a cross-species comparison of neural, cellular, molecular and chemical continuity mechanisms

Do not adopt as:

- proof that memories survive total bodily dissolution
- proof of genetic encoding of acquired memories
- validation of transgenerational inheritance in swallowtails before peer review and replication
- evidence for one universal biological memory substrate
- validation of α = 0.42
