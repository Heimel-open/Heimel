## Source scope

The report presents results from IT i praksis 2026 and frames Norway as being in a transition from experimentation and pilots toward systematic, integrated use of AI in core tasks and services.

The source itself identifies four connected challenges: increasing use with lagging maturity, benefits that are not yet structural at organizational level, growing barriers around competence and risk, and increasing citizen skepticism toward public-sector AI.

The main public-sector survey was conducted February-April 2026 and covers state bodies, municipalities and county municipalities. The report also uses a citizen survey and a private-sector SME survey.

## Empirical signals

### Adoption is moving from experiment toward operational use

- 66% of public-sector respondents reported using AI in 2026.
- Roughly seven in ten public-sector organizations are testing, using or developing AI solutions.
- Current use is still dominated by bounded support tasks such as document analysis, drafting, summarization and information assistants.
- More integrated use is materially less common: 17% report daily AI functionality integrated into professional systems as decision support, 11% daily analysis of complex datasets, and 5% daily predictive use cases.

Interpretation: the Norwegian market is moving from assistant-style AI toward systems that can increasingly affect core workflows, but most organizations have not yet crossed that boundary at scale.

### The constraint is shifting from adoption to governability

The report finds that the dominant barriers are no longer primarily technology, economics or resistance to change. Missing competence and uncertainty about risk are now the leading barriers.

- Nine in ten public-sector organizations experience uncertainty about risk as a barrier at least to some degree.
- Close to four in ten experience risk uncertainty as a large or very large barrier.
- Risk uncertainty increased by 12 percentage points from 2025 to 2026.
- The report explicitly links the growth in these barriers to organizations reaching more concrete and integrated AI use, where privacy, discrimination, security and accountability questions become harder to avoid.

This is important evidence that the production-stage constraint is not simply "can the model work?" but "can the organization safely and legitimately let it participate in consequential work?"

### Regulatory and legal uncertainty is a direct demand signal

Regulatory and legal guidance is the most requested guidance category in the public sector:

- 74% of state organizations request regulatory/legal guidance.
- 71% of municipalities request regulatory/legal guidance.
- 52% of state organizations and 66% of municipalities request guidance on preparing and using data for AI solutions.

The report states that organizations need clarity on what they are actually allowed to do and which risks they must handle. Without that confidence, many stop or slow down.

At the same time, only 15% of public-sector organizations report receiving good support from national authorities to a large or very large degree. The report describes this as a critical national support gap.

Only around one third of organizations already using AI experience national steering signals as clear, and the share is lower among organizations that are not using AI.

### Productivity gains exist, but remain mostly individual

The report finds strong evidence of local productivity benefits while structural organizational benefits lag:

- 83% report faster task completion to some, large or very large degree, up from 61% in 2025.
- 63% report improved quality in processes or services, up from 57%.
- 37% report reduced costs, up from 25%.
- 10% report reduced staffing, up from 6%.

The report explicitly describes the current gains as driven more by behavioral change than system change. Freed capacity is often absorbed into existing workflows instead of being systematically redirected or converted into structural savings.

This creates a measurable value-capture gap: AI can make individuals faster without changing the operating system of the organization.

### Strategy and ownership are weak relative to adoption

The report finds that only half of organizations have a clearly anchored strategic approach to digitalization:

- 27% have integrated digitalization into overall strategy.
- 23% have an established digitalization strategy that they follow systematically.
- 21% work systematically with digitalization but without anchoring it in overall strategy.
- 19% have a strategy but do not work systematically to implement it.
- 9% work ad hoc without strategic anchoring.

The share with clear strategic anchoring fell from 54% in 2025 to 50% in 2026, while the share working systematically without an overarching strategy almost doubled from 11% to 21%.

The report also cites Rambøll's earlier work on generative AI in state organizations, where only one in ten had defined concrete effect goals or expected benefits for AI use.

This matters because execution governance cannot compensate for absent organizational purpose. A system can prove that an action was authorized, but the organization still needs to define what the action is meant to achieve and who owns the outcome.

### Trust is becoming an operational constraint

The source identifies a growing trust gap around public-sector AI.

- 39% of citizens disagree or strongly disagree that public-sector AI should be used as support for decisions about them.
- Among 16–24-year-olds, roughly half disagree or strongly disagree with this use.
- Roughly half of 16–24-year-olds also disagree with public authorities using their data to develop and improve AI services.
- The report finds that general institutional trust does not automatically transfer to AI-enabled public services.

The source argues that public-sector AI therefore cannot be treated only as a technical, legal or efficiency question. Legitimacy, transparency, accountability, purpose and citizen involvement become conditions for durable adoption.

## Five structural gaps exposed by the report

This section is VALO interpretation, not Rambøll's terminology.

### 1. Adoption gap -> maturity gap

The first bottleneck was getting organizations to use AI. That bottleneck is weakening. The next bottleneck is whether AI can become safely integrated into core processes.

### 2. Guidance gap -> execution gap

Organizations ask for regulation, checklists, templates and sandboxes. Those mechanisms improve preparedness, but they do not answer the runtime question for one concrete action.

Static guidance says what should generally be allowed. Execution governance determines whether this action is admissible now.

### 3. Productivity gap -> value-capture gap

83% faster task completion versus 37% reduced costs is not evidence that AI fails. It is evidence that organizations have not yet redesigned workflows, responsibilities and resource allocation around the new capability.

The transition from personal productivity to organizational productivity requires governed workflow change, not merely more model access.

### 4. Institutional trust -> demonstrable trust

Norway's high institutional trust is not inherited automatically by AI systems. Trust has to be supported by visible mechanisms: purpose, authority, traceability, human responsibility, evidence, appealability and proof of what actually happened.

### 5. Policy intent -> operational control

The report repeatedly calls for clearer national direction. Direction alone does not create control. A policy objective only becomes operationally meaningful when it can constrain concrete execution.

This is the boundary between governance as text and governance as executable infrastructure.

## The maturity paradox

A particularly important signal in the report is that adoption and uncertainty rise at the same time.

That should not automatically be interpreted as governance getting worse. It can mean organizations have moved far enough beyond experimentation to discover the real governance problem.

At low maturity, the question is:

`Can we use AI?`

At higher maturity, the questions become:

`May this system do this?`

`Under whose authority?`

`For what purpose?`

`Using which evidence?`

`Under which current conditions?`

`Who remains accountable?`

`Can we prove afterwards what was authorized and what actually happened?`

This is a strong empirical transition signal for execution governance.

## VALO relevance

The report is strong Norwegian empirical validation for the transition VALO is designed around:

`pilot / assistant use -> integrated AI -> consequential action -> execution governance`

The source documents the first three elements of that transition well: adoption is rising, integrated use is the intended next step, and uncertainty rises as AI approaches core processes and decisions.

What the report does not specify is a runtime mechanism that can determine, for each concrete proposed action, whether execution is admissible under the current authority, mandate, purpose, evidence, policy, scope and state.

That missing layer is the execution-governance gap.

## Execution-governance interpretation

This is a VALO interpretation, not a claim made by Rambøll.

Guidance, checklists, competence programs, regulatory sandboxes and documentation support can improve organizational preparedness. They do not by themselves establish whether one specific action is legitimate at the exact moment of execution.

For integrated or agentic systems, the required chain becomes:

`guidance / law / policy -> authority and purpose state -> evaluation -> commit-time authorization -> deterministic decision expression -> enforcement -> post-execution evidence`

Mapped to current VALO architecture:

`Authority State -> VAIG -> reht -> RACS -> external enforcement -> Veritas`

This distinction becomes more important as AI moves from drafting and recommendation into mutations such as changing case state, updating registers, granting or denying benefits, triggering payments, reserving resources, modifying access, or otherwise affecting rights and obligations.

## Purpose, benefit and legitimacy should be connected

The report repeatedly asks a deeper governance question: why is AI being used, what problem is being solved, what value is intended, and how should efficiency be balanced against privacy, legal safeguards and democratic control?

That supports treating purpose as a first-class governed input rather than narrative metadata.

VALO interpretation:

`purpose -> expected benefit -> authorized activity -> observed outcome -> evidence`

This creates a closed governance loop instead of treating compliance and benefit realization as separate management exercises.

A concrete action should not only be technically possible and legally admissible. The system should also be able to relate the action to an approved organizational purpose and retain evidence that allows the organization to evaluate whether the intended benefit was actually produced.

## Implication for Human in the Lead

The trust findings argue against a simplistic "human in the loop" checkbox.

Human responsibility needs to remain structurally legible:

- who set the purpose;
- who holds the mandate;
- what was delegated to the machine;
- which decisions remain reserved for humans;
- when the machine must defer or step up;
- who can revoke authority;
- who owns the resulting outcome.

A human clicking approve after an opaque process is not equivalent to governed human leadership.

## Implication for public-sector deployment

The report suggests a practical deployment ladder:

1. Assistant use: drafting, summarization, search.
2. Decision support: AI influences professional judgment but does not mutate authoritative state.
3. Governed workflow participation: AI can prepare or propose state-changing operations under explicit scope.
4. Authorized execution: concrete mutations require current authority, purpose, policy, evidence and state validation at the execution boundary.
5. Continuous assurance: outcomes and receipts feed monitoring, audit, learning and policy improvement.

The report directly observes stages 1-2 and growing interest in deeper integration. VALO's relevance begins to become material at the transition from 2 to 3 and becomes critical at 4.

## Testable research hypotheses derived from the source

These are VALO hypotheses for future validation, not findings claimed by Rambøll.

H1: As AI use moves closer to authoritative state change, perceived risk uncertainty rises unless runtime governance mechanisms are present.

H2: Organizations with explicit purpose, authority and execution controls will move from pilot to production faster than organizations relying primarily on policy documents and manual review.

H3: Receipt-backed execution will improve auditability and organizational confidence without requiring blanket human approval for every low-risk action.

H4: Structural productivity gains will correlate more strongly with governed workflow redesign than with model capability alone.

H5: Public trust will be higher where organizations can explain not only which model was used, but why an action was permitted, who authorized the mandate, what evidence supported it and what actually happened.

These hypotheses can be tested in shadow-mode deployments and public-sector pilots.

- evidence of a value-capture gap between individual productivity and organizational transformation;

- a Norwegian public-sector evidence source for ACEG / governed execution positioning.

## Canonical questions

Primary:

When AI moves from helping a public employee produce work to participating directly in a consequential workflow, how does the organization prove that each concrete action was legitimate, authorized and supported by valid evidence at the exact moment it executed?

Secondary:

If AI makes 83% of organizations faster but only 37% report lower costs, what governance and workflow changes are required to convert individual productivity into measurable organizational value without sacrificing legitimacy and control?
